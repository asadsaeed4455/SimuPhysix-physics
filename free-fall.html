Free Fall

Difficulty: BeginnerKinematics

An object in free fall moves under gravity alone. All objects then share the same acceleration g, whatever their mass. Drop, throw up or throw down an object, switch on air resistance, and see how every quantity changes.

Learning objectives
  • Apply the equations of motion with constant acceleration a = −g, using a consistent sign convention.
  • Find fall time, maximum height and impact speed for dropped, upward and downward throws.
  • Explain why mass does not affect free fall and how air resistance changes that.
  • Describe terminal velocity and read position, velocity, acceleration and energy graphs.

Complete Physics Lecture

1. Introduction

Drop a coin and it speeds up as it falls. Throw it up and it slows, stops for an instant, then comes back down. Both motions are governed by one fact: near a planet's surface, gravity gives every object the same acceleration. Galileo showed this around 1600, overturning the old belief that heavy objects fall faster.

2. Definition

Free fall is the motion of an object on which gravity is the only force acting. The object may be moving up, down, or momentarily at rest. What matters is that its acceleration is g (about 9.8 m/s² on Earth), directed downward, at every instant.

3. Physical principles

  • Same acceleration for all masses. Weight is W = mg and Newton's second law gives a = W/m = g. The mass cancels (gravitational and inertial mass are equal).
  • Constant acceleration. Near the surface g changes very little, so the equations for uniformly accelerated motion apply.
  • Direction matters. Velocity can point up or down, but the acceleration always points down, even at the top of the path.
  • Energy conservation. Without air resistance, KE + PE is constant: ½mv² + mgy = constant.
  • Air resistance. Drag grows with speed, so a falling object eventually reaches a terminal velocity where drag equals weight and the acceleration becomes zero.

4. Step-by-step method

  1. Choose the origin and direction. Here y is height above the ground and up is positive.
  2. List known quantities with signs: h₀, v₀ (positive if thrown up, negative if thrown down) and a = −g.
  3. Choose the equation that contains the unknown and no unwanted variable.
  4. To find the landing time, set y = 0 and keep the positive root.
  5. Check the sign of your final velocity: a downward result should be negative.

7. Derivations

Velocity and position. Since a = dv/dt = −g is constant, integrating gives v = v₀ − gt. Integrating v = dy/dt gives y = h₀ + v₀t − ½gt².

Velocity–position relation. From v = v₀ − gt, t = (v₀ − v)/g. Substituting into y − h₀ = v₀t − ½gt² gives y − h₀ = (v₀ − v)(v₀ + v)/(2g) = (v₀² − v²)/(2g), so v² = v₀² − 2g(y − h₀).

Dropped from rest. Falling from h₀ to the ground, h₀ = ½gT², so T = √(2h₀/g). The speed is v = gT = √(2gh₀).

Throwing upward. At the top v = 0, so tapex = v₀/g and H = h₀ + v₀²/(2g). Landing happens when y = 0, i.e. ½gT² − v₀T − h₀ = 0. Its positive root is T = [v₀ + √(v₀² + 2gh₀)]/g. Putting y = 0 into the v² equation gives the impact speed √(v₀² + 2gh₀), which does not depend on whether the object was thrown up or down.

Terminal velocity. Drag F = ½ρCdAv² balances weight mg when ½ρCdAvt² = mg, so vt = √(2mg/(ρCdA)) = √(g/k). For a falling object with speed s, ds/dt = g − ks². With s(0) = 0 the solution is s = vt tanh(gt/vt), which approaches vt and never exceeds it. The distance fallen is d = (vt²/g) ln cosh(gt/vt).

Energy. d(½v²)/dt = v·a = −gv = −g·dy/dt = −d(gy)/dt, so ½v² + gy is constant per kilogram. With drag, this total decreases because drag turns mechanical energy into heat in the air.

8. Physical interpretation

Every second, the downward speed grows by 9.8 m/s. Starting from rest, the distances fallen in successive seconds are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 (4.9 m, 14.7 m, 24.5 m, ...), because the speed keeps growing. A ball thrown up spends the same time rising as it does falling back to its launch level, and it returns with the same speed it left with. On the Moon there is no air, and in 1971 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather together and they hit the ground at the same time.

9. Everyday applications

Skydiving and parachutes (terminal velocity), drop towers and amusement-park rides, weightlessness in parabolic flights and on orbit, measuring g in laboratories, estimating a well's depth from a splash time, and the ruler-drop reaction-time test (d = ½gt²).

10. Common misconceptions

  • "Heavier objects fall faster." Only air resistance makes a difference. In a vacuum a feather and a hammer fall together.
  • "At the top of the path the acceleration is zero." No. The velocity is zero for an instant, but a = −g all the time.
  • "The acceleration decreases as the object falls." Without drag it stays exactly constant. With drag it does decrease, and reaches zero at terminal speed.
  • "Astronauts feel weightless because there is no gravity." Gravity there is still about 90% of its surface value. They are in free fall around the Earth.
  • "A rising object must have an upward acceleration." Velocity and acceleration are different. The object rises with a downward acceleration, so it slows.

11. What to observe in the simulation

  • Drop from 50 m with v₀ = 0 and look at the strobe circles: the gaps increase steadily.
  • Set v₀ = +20 m/s. Pause at the top: v = 0 but the green arrow (a) still points down.
  • Compare v₀ = +15 and v₀ = −15 from the same height: the impact speeds are equal.
  • Switch gravity to the Moon: fall time rises by a factor √(9.8/1.62) ≈ 2.5.
  • Turn on air resistance with the Skydiver preset and h₀ = 500 m. The speed levels off near vt and a goes to zero. The dashed vacuum ball hits the ground much sooner.

Worked Numerical Examples

Example 1: Stone dropped from a tower

  1. Given: h₀ = 80 m, v₀ = 0, g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Required: time to reach the ground and impact speed.
  3. Equations: h₀ = ½gT² and v = gT (check with v = √(2gh₀)).
  4. Substitution: T = √(2 × 80 / 9.8) = √16.33.
  5. Calculation: T = 4.04 s, and v = 9.8 × 4.04 = 39.6 m/s. Check: √(2 × 9.8 × 80) = √1568 = 39.6 m/s ✓
Answer: T = 4.04 s, impact speed 39.6 m/s (directed downward).

Example 2: Ball thrown upward from a building

  1. Given: h₀ = 25 m, v₀ = +20 m/s (up), g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Required: maximum height above the ground, time to reach the ground, impact speed.
  3. Equations: H = h₀ + v₀²/(2g); T = [v₀ + √(v₀² + 2gh₀)]/g; v = √(v₀² + 2gh₀).
  4. Height: H = 25 + 20²/(2 × 9.8) = 25 + 400/19.6 = 25 + 20.41 = 45.4 m.
  5. Discriminant: v₀² + 2gh₀ = 400 + 2 × 9.8 × 25 = 890, √890 = 29.83 m/s.
  6. Time: T = (20 + 29.83)/9.8 = 5.08 s.
  7. Impact speed: |v| = 29.8 m/s, downward.
Answer: H = 45.4 m, T = 5.08 s, impact speed 29.8 m/s.

Example 3: Terminal speed of a skydiver

  1. Given: m = 80 kg, Cd = 1.0, A = 0.70 m², ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Required: terminal speed vt.
  3. Equation: vt = √(2mg / (ρCdA)).
  4. Substitution: vt = √(2 × 80 × 9.8 / (1.2 × 1.0 × 0.70)).
  5. Calculation: numerator = 1568, denominator = 0.84, ratio = 1866.7, so vt = √1866.7 = 43.2 m/s.
Answer: vt ≈ 43 m/s (about 156 km/h). Equivalent k = ρCdA/2m = 0.0053 m⁻¹.

Learning Assessment

Conceptual questions

  1. Why do a hammer and a feather fall together on the Moon but not on Earth?
  2. A ball is thrown straight up. What are its velocity and acceleration at the highest point?
  3. Does the acceleration of an object dropped from rest (no air) change as it falls? Explain.
  4. A ball thrown upward returns to the thrower's hand. Compare its launch speed with its return speed, and the time going up with the time coming down.
  5. Astronauts in orbit appear weightless even though gravity still acts on them. Why?

Numerical questions (take g = 9.8 m/s² unless stated)

  1. A stone is dropped from a 45 m cliff. Find the time to reach the ground and the impact speed.
  2. A ball is thrown upward from the ground at 15 m/s. Find the maximum height and total time of flight.
  3. An object is dropped from rest. How far has it fallen after 3.0 s, and how fast is it moving?
  4. A ball is dropped from 10 m on the Moon (g = 1.62 m/s²). How long does it take to land?
  5. A stone is thrown downward at 10 m/s from a 60 m high bridge. Find its impact speed and time of fall.
Show answers and explanations

Conceptual

  1. The Moon has no atmosphere, so only gravity acts and both fall with the same acceleration. On Earth air resistance affects the light, wide feather far more than the dense hammer.
  2. Velocity is zero for an instant. Acceleration is still g = 9.8 m/s² downward.
  3. No. With only gravity acting, a = −g is constant, so the speed grows by the same amount every second.
  4. The return speed equals the launch speed, and the times up and down are equal. This follows from energy conservation and the symmetry of constant acceleration.
  5. Gravity is what keeps them in orbit. They and their spacecraft are both in free fall with the same acceleration, so nothing pushes against them and they feel no contact force, which is what we feel as weight.

Numerical

  1. T = √(2 × 45 / 9.8) = √9.18 = 3.03 s; v = gT = 9.8 × 3.03 = 29.7 m/s.
  2. H = v₀²/(2g) = 225 / 19.6 = 11.5 m; T = 2v₀/g = 30 / 9.8 = 3.06 s.
  3. d = ½gt² = 0.5 × 9.8 × 9 = 44.1 m; v = gt = 9.8 × 3.0 = 29.4 m/s.
  4. t = √(2 × 10 / 1.62) = √12.35 = 3.51 s.
  5. v = √(10² + 2 × 9.8 × 60) = √1276 = 35.7 m/s. Time: t = (v − v₀)/g = (35.7 − 10)/9.8 = 2.62 s.
